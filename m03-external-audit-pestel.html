Where This Fits

In the strategic-management model, the External Assessment (Chapter 3) sits beside the Internal Assessment (Chapter 4), both feeding strategy formulation after the vision and mission are set. This lecture covers the first half of the external assessment — PESTEL — and Lecture 4 continues with the competitive side.

External forces can be so important that identifying them may lead a firm to revise its vision and mission statements, design strategies to achieve long-term objectives, and develop policies to achieve annual objectives.

The External Audit

External audit
Focuses on identifying and evaluating trends and events beyond the control of a single firm. It reveals the key opportunities and threats confronting an organization so that managers can formulate strategies to take advantage of the opportunities and avoid or reduce the impact of threats. Sometimes called industry analysis.
🎯 Its Purpose
  • Identify key variables that offer actionable responses
  • Let the firm respond offensively or defensively — strategies that take advantage of opportunities, or minimize the impact of threats
  • Select only factors that relate closely to the firm achieving its mission (opportunities) or hindering it (threats): the factors chosen should be driven by the company's mission

Opportunity

An external factor that helps the firm achieve its mission — something to take advantage of.

Threat

An external factor that hinders the firm's mission — something to avoid or whose impact must be reduced.

PESTEL & Five Forces Together

🎯 The Two Lenses of the External Audit (Figure 3.2)

The textbook's Figure 3.2 shows two concentric rings around the organization's opportunities and threats. The outer ring is PESTEL (the broad forces); the inner ring is competitive analysis and Porter's Five Forces (the industry forces closest to the firm). Both rings point to the same centre: the firm's opportunities and threats.

How to Use These Numbers

25 + 20 + 55 = 100. Read the ordering, not the decimals: external factors together account for under half of performance and internal factors for more than half — which is why the external audit never replaces the internal one. The lecture presents these percentages without a source on the slide, so quote them as "per the lecture" in an exam.

  • Five Forces is more refined than PESTEL and focuses on key industry stakeholders
  • Combined with PESTEL, it helps the firm discover key opportunities and threats

The AQCD Test

When identifying and prioritizing the essential PESTEL and Five Forces factors, four criteria measure the quality of an external factor.

A — Actionable

Meaningful and helpful in ultimately deciding what actions or strategies the firm should consider pursuing.

Q — Quantitative

Includes percentages, ratios, dollars, and numbers to the extent possible.

C — Comparative

Reveals changes over time.

D — Divisional

Relates to the firm's products or regions (rather than consolidated), so inferences can be drawn about what products and regions are doing well or not.

⚖️ Scoring a Factor
High quality: meets 3 or 4 of the AQCD criteria. Low quality: meets two or fewer.
Walmart exampleActionableQuantitativeComparativeDivisionalQuality
"Online retail grocery shopping grew from 12% to 16% in 2022."yesyesyesyesHigh (4/4)
"Consumers' average disposable incomes increased greatly in 2022."nonononoLow (0/4)
Why the First Passes and the Second Fails

The first factor has numbers (12% → 16%), shows change over time, and is specific to one part of the business (grocery, online), so Walmart can act on it. The second is vague ("greatly"), has no figures, no time comparison, and is not tied to any product or region — it tells management nothing to do. When you write PESTEL points for the mid-term, rewrite weak factors until they pass.

How External Forces Affect the Firm

Changes in external forces translate into changes in demand for both industrial and consumer goods and services. External forces affect:

  • Types of products developed and services offered
  • The nature of market segmentation
  • Positioning strategies
  • The choice of businesses to acquire or sell
They also have a direct impact on both suppliers and distributors.

The Six PESTEL Forces

🏛️ P — Political

Local, state, and federal laws, regulatory agencies, and special-interest groups can have a major impact on the strategies of small, large, for-profit, and nonprofit organizations.

Aspects to examine

  • Political stability
  • Foreign policy
  • Security and rule of law
  • Corruption and transparency
  • Election and political cycle
  • Legislation
  • International credibility

Examples from the lecture

  • Seven U.S. states have zero state income taxes, which makes them attractive for locating facilities
  • Between 2016 and 2020 FedEx invested $71 million on campaign contributions and lobbying, and received tax credits of $877 million between 2018 and 2020
  • Dubai's reforms — zero tax rates, alcohol consumption without a license, and others — made it one of the most booming economies in the world
💰 E — Economic

Economic factors have a direct impact on the potential attractiveness of various strategies.

Example: a strong U.S. dollar can boost profits of importers but harms large multinational firms, since it makes U.S. goods more expensive to foreign buyers. In the first four months of 2022, the dollar rose 10 percent against the Japanese yen and more than 5 percent against the euro.
Factors listed in the lecture
Shift to a service economy · availability of credit · level of disposable income (income available to spend after tax deductions) · propensity (inclination) of people to spend · interest rates · inflation rates · GDP trends · import/export factors · unemployment trends · value of the dollar in world markets
Demand shifts for different goods and services · income differences by region and consumer groups · price fluctuations · foreign countries' economic conditions · monetary and fiscal policies · stock market trends · tax rate variation by country and state · European Economic Community (EEC) policies · OPEC policies
👥 S — Sociocultural

Sociocultural forces impact strategic decisions on virtually all products, services, markets, and customers, and shape the way people live, work, produce, and consume.

Examples: declining demand for packaged foods as people want healthier and fresher options; the ongoing shift to online shopping; the aging U.S. population.
Factors listed in the lecture
Population changes by race, age, and geographic area · regional changes in tastes and preferences · number of marriages, divorces, births, and deaths · immigration and emigration rates · Social Security programs
Life expectancy rates · per capita income · social media pervasiveness · attitudes toward retirement, product quality, and customer service · social programs · social responsibility issues
🤖 T — Technological

A variety of new technologies impact strategic planning decisions, including:

  • The Internet of Things (IoT)
  • Three-dimensional (3D) printing
  • Predictive analytics
  • Quantum computing
  • Robotics
  • Artificial intelligence (AI)
  • The increase in the amount of data

🌍 E — Environmental

The second E in PESTEL stands for environmental forces.

Examples of forces

  • Climate change
  • Carbon dioxide (CO₂) emissions
  • Pollution abatement
  • Water quality
  • Air quality
  • Ecological factors
  • Weather

Trends shaping business

  • Rapid electrification of vehicles
  • Growing pollution problem around the world
  • Shift from AC power to DC power that can be stored
  • Solar, wind, and hydrogen power sources expanding
  • Executive compensation increasingly tied to ESG metrics
⚖️ L — Legal

Legal forces include all the regulations and laws imposed on various businesses globally, such as:

  • Mandatory minimum wage
  • Labor laws
  • Consumer laws
  • Antitrust laws
  • Changing tax and accounting regulations

Telling PESTEL Forces Apart

Most lost marks in a PESTEL come from putting one fact under the wrong heading or listing the same fact twice. The lecture gives two tests.

🔀 Political vs. Legal — the Decision vs. the Law
ExamplePolitical (the decision)Legal (the instrument)
Price controlGovernment decides to cap pricesThe actual decree or law that enforces the cap
TaxationDecision to increase VATThe VAT law, compliance requirements, and penalties
Rule of thumb (study aid): Political = what government chooses to do. Legal = the rule that binds the firm as a result.
🔗 Source vs. Impact — Cause and Effect Across Forces

A force's source (where it starts) is not the same as its impact (what it does to the firm). The lecture pairs a political source with an economic impact:

ThemePolitical (source)Economic (impact)
Government intervention vs. inflationGovernment imposes price controls on essential goodsInflation reduces purchasing power and increases price sensitivity
State development vs. demand patternsGovernment invests in new cities and infrastructureUrbanization increases demand for modern retail formats
Avoid Double Counting (study aid)

In a PESTEL, list the source once under its own force and explain the effect in the "how it influences strategy" line. Do not repeat the same development under both Political and Economic as if it were two separate factors.

Exam Preparation

Q1: What is an external audit and what is its purpose?

The external audit identifies and evaluates trends and events beyond the control of a single firm. It reveals key opportunities and threats so managers can formulate strategies to take advantage of opportunities and avoid or reduce the impact of threats. It is sometimes called industry analysis, and the factors chosen should be driven by the firm's mission.

Q2: List the six PESTEL forces and give one example of each.

Political (e.g., zero state income tax); Economic (e.g., a strong dollar raising import profits but hurting multinationals); Sociocultural (e.g., demand shifting to healthier, fresher food); Technological (e.g., AI or the Internet of Things); Environmental (e.g., climate change, ESG-linked executive pay); Legal (e.g., antitrust or minimum-wage laws).

Q3: Explain the AQCD test and apply it to: "Consumers' average disposable incomes increased greatly in 2022."

AQCD judges the quality of an external factor: Actionable, Quantitative, Comparative, Divisional. A factor is high quality if it meets 3 or 4, low quality if two or fewer. The disposable-income statement meets none — it is vague, has no numbers, shows no comparison over time, and is not tied to a product or region — so it is a low-quality factor (0 of 4). A rewrite such as the lecture's "online retail grocery shopping grew from 12% to 16% in 2022" passes all four.

💡 Always state the count (e.g., "0 of 4") and the verdict, not just yes/no per letter.
Q4: How do PESTEL and Five Forces relate? How much of firm performance does each explain?

They are two lenses on the same opportunities and threats: PESTEL is the broad outer ring of general forces; Five Forces is the more refined inner ring focused on key industry stakeholders (current competitors, potential entrants, suppliers, buyers, substitutes). Per the lecture, PESTEL can account for up to 25% of performance, Five Forces another 20%, and the remaining 55% is explained by internal factors.

Q5: Government caps the price of essential goods, then issues a decree enforcing the cap. How would you classify the two events in a PESTEL?

The decision to cap prices is a Political force; the decree or law that enforces it is a Legal force. The economic consequence — inflation eroding purchasing power and raising price sensitivity — is an Economic impact of the political source. List each once, under its own force.

Q6: Why should the factors in an external assessment be driven by the firm's mission?

Because opportunities are factors that help the firm achieve its mission and threats are factors that hinder it. A factor unrelated to the mission offers no actionable response, so including it only dilutes the analysis.

Glossary

External Audit
Identification and evaluation of trends and events beyond a single firm's control; reveals opportunities and threats. Also called industry analysis.
Opportunity
An external factor that helps the firm achieve its mission.
Threat
An external factor that hinders the firm's mission.
PESTEL
Acronym for the six external force groups: Political, Economic, Sociocultural, Technological, Environmental, Legal.
Five-Forces Analysis
A more refined analysis of key industry stakeholders: current competitors, potential new competitors, suppliers, buyers, substitute products.
AQCD Test
A measure of the quality of an external factor: Actionable, Quantitative, Comparative, Divisional. High quality means 3 or 4 criteria met.
Black Swan Event
An unexpected high-impact external event, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, that PESTEL can account for.
Disposable Income
Income available to spend after tax deductions.
Propensity to Spend
The inclination of people to spend.
ESG
Environmental, social, and governance metrics; the lecture notes executive compensation is increasingly tied to them.
