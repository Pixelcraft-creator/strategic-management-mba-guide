Where This Fits

Module 3 introduced the external audit and its two lenses: PESTEL (the broad outer ring) and Porter's Five Forces (the inner ring of industry stakeholders). This module develops the inner ring, then shows how both lenses are summarised into a single scored table — the EFE matrix — and how the firm is benchmarked against rivals in the CPM.

Flow of the mid-term analysis: PESTEL → Five Forces → EFE matrix → CPM. Each step feeds the next, and the EFE matrix is where the PESTEL and Five-Forces factors are finally weighted and rated.

Knowing Your Competitors

An important part of an external audit is identifying rival firms and determining their strengths, weaknesses, capabilities, opportunities, threats, objectives, and strategies. The lecture opens with a warning (George Salk): "If you're not faster than your competitor, you're in a tenuous [weak] position, and if you're only half as fast, you're terminal."

❓ Ten Key Questions About Competitors
#Question
1What are the strengths and weaknesses of our major competitors?
2What products and services do we offer that are unique in the industry?
3What are the objectives and strategies of our major competitors?
4How will our major competitors most likely respond to current political, economic, sociocultural, technological, environmental, and legal trends affecting our industry?
5How vulnerable are our major competitors to our new strategies, products, and services?
6How vulnerable is our firm to successful counterattack by our major competitors?
7How does our firm compare to rivals in mastering the social media conversation in this industry?
8To what extent are new firms entering and old firms leaving this industry?
9What key factors have resulted in our present competitive position in this industry?
10How are supplier and distributor relationships changing in this industry?

Competitive Intelligence

Competitive intelligence (CI)
A systematic and ethical process for gathering and analyzing information about the competition's activities and general business trends to further a business's own goals. Quality CI, in business as in the military, is one of the keys to success.
Turn rivals' profiles into factors: major competitors' weaknesses can represent external opportunities; major competitors' strengths may represent key threats.
🔎 Legal and Ethical Ways to Obtain CI
  • Reverse-engineer rival firms' products
  • Surveys and interviews of customers, suppliers, and distributors of rival firms
  • Analyze a rival's Form 10-K (US)
  • Fly-over and drive-by visits to rival operations
  • Search online databases
  • Contact government agencies for public information about rivals
  • Monitor trade publications, magazines, and newspapers
  • Purchase social-media data about customers of all firms in the industry
  • Hire top executives from rival firms

Porter's Five Forces

🕸️ The Model (Figure 3.3)

Rivalry among competing firms sits at the centre of the diagram, with the other four forces around it: bargaining power of suppliers (above), bargaining power of consumers (below), potential entry of new competitors (left), and potential development of substitute products (right).

Porter (2008), quoted on the slide: the five forces govern the profit structure of an industry by determining how the economic value it creates is apportioned. Value can be drained by rivalry among existing competitors, bargained away through the power of suppliers or customers, or constrained by the threat of new entrants or substitutes. Strategy can be viewed as building defenses against the competitive forces or as finding a position in an industry where the forces are weaker.
⚔️ 1 · Rivalry Among Competing Firms

The most powerful of the five forces. It focuses on the competitive advantage of strategies over other firms. Intense rivalry can decrease overall industry profits, because firms often lower prices or spend extra on advertising to hold market share — often transferring profits directly to consumers and other players in the model.

Regional examples on the slide: telecom, real estate, banking, FMCG, hospitality, ride-hailing services.

🏭 2 · Bargaining Power of Suppliers

Supplier power is increased when there are:

  • Few suppliers
  • Few substitutes
  • High costs of switching raw materials

Examples on the slide: oil and gas (Aramco, Schlumberger drilling equipment); pharma (ingredients and APIs); construction (steel, cement); aerospace and defense (high reliance on few suppliers).

Backward integration
Gaining control or ownership of suppliers — a firm's response to strong supplier power.

Saudi Aramco

Invested heavily in its own refining and petrochemical facilities, such as SABIC (Saudi Basic Industries Corporation), which it acquired in 2020. Producing its own petrochemical feedstocks and refining its own crude reduces reliance on third-party processors. Reason: to mitigate dependency on international refining and chemical processing partners.

Almarai

Invested in owning and operating its own dairy farms, feed production facilities, and grain farming operations abroad (e.g., in Argentina and the USA). Reason: to reduce dependence on external feed suppliers and manage costs in dairy production.

🛒 3 · Bargaining Power of Consumers

Consumers gain bargaining power:

  • If they can inexpensively switch to competing brands or substitutes
  • If they are particularly important to the seller
  • If sellers are struggling in the face of falling consumer demand
  • If they are informed about sellers' products, prices, and costs
  • If they have discretion in whether and when they purchase the product

Slide prompt: retail or ride-hailing services (consumer power high) versus utilities, education, pharma (lower) — the slide poses this as a question to discuss, not as a settled answer.

🚪 4 · Potential Entry of New Competitors

Barriers to entry are important, though quality, pricing, and marketing can overcome them.

Examples on the slide: low barriers — e-commerce and online retail (Amazon, Noon, Jumia), restaurants and cloud kitchens, digital wallets, lending platforms. Low potential — oil and gas, aviation, steel manufacturing.

Deterring entrants by consolidating: Amazon acquired Whole Foods and Zappos while investing heavily in logistics and delivery infrastructure; Cleopatra Hospitals acquired hospitals (Elsherouk, Nile Badrawy); Edita acquired smaller brands and partnered on others (Hohos, Hostess).

🔄 5 · Potential Development of Substitute Products

Competitive pressure from substitutes increases when the prices of substitutes decrease and when consumers' switching costs decrease.

Examples on the slide: government moving to solar versus oil; airplane versus other transport; cooking versus food ordering (meal kits); public transport versus ride-hailing; traditional banking versus fintech; calls versus VoIP and messaging. Industries named as exposed: oil, utilities, healthcare, aviation, construction materials, and tourist and pilgrimage sites.

Five-Forces Pitfalls

1
Equal weight on all five forces
Instead, identify the most pressing forces for your industry.
2
Defining the industry too broad or too narrow
The boundary you draw changes every force.
3
Labelling the industry "attractive" or "unattractive"
Use the model to formulate strategies more efficiently, not to pin labels on an industry.
When using the model, strive to identify AQCD opportunities and threats that are most important for success in the industry and most relevant to the firm's vision and mission.

The EFE Matrix

External Factor Evaluation (EFE) Matrix
A widely used strategic-planning analytical tool designed to summarize and evaluate economic, social, cultural, demographic, environmental, political, governmental, legal, technological, and competitive information. It gives an empirical assessment of how well a firm is handling external factors overall, including its effectiveness at capitalizing on opportunities and minimizing threats.
🧮 The Five Steps
1
List key external factors
Develop and narrow to 20 — 10 opportunities (O) and 10 threats (T).
2
Assign each factor a weight
From 0.0 (not important) to 1.0 (very important); weights sum to 1.0.
3
Rate how effectively current strategies respond
Scale of 1 to 4.
4
Multiply weight × rating
This gives the weighted score for each factor.
5
Sum the weighted scores
The total weighted score measures overall effectiveness.
💡 Tips From the Lecture

Building the factor list

  • Brainstorm (the lecture suggests the Delphi method)
  • Compile two data sets, opportunities and threats — perhaps 50 each at first, applying AQCD as far as possible
  • Include factors most important to the industry given the firm's vision, mission, and strategies; consider PESTEL and the Five Forces
  • Narrow to 20 (10 O first, then 10 T); rank with Delphi if you like
  • Do not include strategies as opportunities — building a factory is a strategy; an increase in demand is an opportunity
  • Make sure factors are distinct, not repeated

Weights, ratings, and the total

  • Weights range from 0.01 to 1.00 and show relative importance for success in the industry (0.03 is three times 0.01). O and T need not split weights equally
  • Ratings (4 to 1) reflect how effectively the firm's strategies capitalize on an opportunity or avoid and mitigate a threat. Ratings are company-based, not industry-based
  • Total weighted score runs from lowest 1 to highest 4; average 2.5
  • A total of 3.5, for example, means the firm's strategies effectively take advantage of opportunities and minimize the adverse effects of threats

The slides head both tips with a quotation: "In God we trust. Everyone else brings data" (attributed to W. E. Deming) — the reason AQCD factors, with numbers, are preferred.

2.58 is just above the 2.5 average. The cinema's strategies are responding to its external environment slightly better than average, but not strongly. Look at why: its best-handled threat is the healthy-eating trend (weight 0.12, rating 4 → 0.48, the largest single score), while its worst-handled opportunity is the new neighborhoods (weight 0.09, rating only 1). The Competitive Profile Matrix

Competitive Profile Matrix (CPM)
A widely used strategic-planning analytical tool designed to identify a firm's major competitors and their particular strengths and weaknesses in relation to a sample firm's strategic position. It reveals how a firm compares with major competitors across a range of key factors and gives its competitive advantages or disadvantages in an industry.
📐 Rules for Building a CPM
  • Tailor factors to the industry. In airlines, on-time arrival, leg room, and routes served are far better than vague factors like "quality of service" or "financial condition"
  • Weights are industry-based and sum to 1.0
  • Ratings quantify how well the firm and each competitor are doing on each critical success factor — 4 superior, 3 above average, 2 average, 1 poor
  • Avoid giving the same rating to two or more firms on a factor, since the purpose is to differentiate between firms
  • The template asks for 12 factors; the textbook example uses only 8 for simplicity and says that is really too few

Net Promoter Score

The lecture introduces Net Promoter Score (NPS) as a way to get customer-based ratings for a CPM. Respondents answer on a 1–10 scale:

9–10 · Promoters

They drive growth: enthusiastic advocates who bring in new customers through recommendations.

7–8 · Passives

At risk: not actively harmful, but indifferent and prone to leaving for competitors.

0–6 · Detractors

They damage the brand: they can harm your reputation and discourage potential customers.

Net Promoter Score
NPS = % Promoters − % Detractors
Passives are ignored in the subtraction. Result ranges from −100 to +100 (my statement; the slide only shows the example).
Slide example: out of 100 respondents, 50 promoters, 20 detractors, 30 passives → NPS = 50 − 20 = 30 (recomputed and confirmed). Action implied: work on turning passives into promoters.

Exam Preparation

Q1: Name and explain Porter's five forces. Which is the most powerful?

Rivalry among competing firms (the most powerful force), bargaining power of suppliers, bargaining power of consumers, potential entry of new competitors, and potential development of substitute products. Together they govern the profit structure of an industry by determining how the economic value it creates is apportioned.

Q2: State five conditions that cause high rivalry among competing firms.

Any five of: many competing firms; firms of similar size; similar capabilities; rapidly changing demand; common price cuts; easy brand switching; high exit barriers; low entry barriers; high fixed costs; perishable products or short life cycles.

Q3: What is backward integration? Give an example and the reason for it.

Gaining control or ownership of suppliers. Example: Almarai owns dairy farms, feed production, and grain farming abroad to reduce dependence on external feed suppliers and manage costs. Saudi Aramco's investment in refining and petrochemicals (including SABIC) reduces reliance on third-party processors.

Q4: Describe the steps for building an EFE matrix and interpret a total weighted score of 3.5 and of 2.58.

Steps: list the key external factors (10 O and 10 T); weight each from 0.0 to 1.0 (summing to 1.0); rate each from 1 to 4 on how effectively current strategies respond; multiply weight by rating; sum the weighted scores. Totals run from 1 to 4 with an average of 2.5. A 3.5 means the firm's strategies effectively take advantage of opportunities and minimize the adverse effects of threats; a 2.58 is only slightly above average — a modestly effective response.

💡 Always say "above or below the 2.5 average" and by how much.
Q5: Which of the following should NOT be listed as an opportunity in an EFE matrix: "Demand for online grocery grows 16%" or "Build a new distribution centre"? Why?

"Build a new distribution centre" is a strategy, not an opportunity. The lecture's own example: building a factory is a strategy, an increase in demand is an opportunity. Opportunities and threats are external factors beyond the firm's control; strategies are the firm's response to them.

Q6: Compute the CPM totals: weights 0.50 / 0.30 / 0.20; Firm A ratings 4 / 2 / 3; Firm B ratings 2 / 3 / 4. Who leads?

Firm A: 0.50×4 + 0.30×2 + 0.20×3 = 2.00 + 0.60 + 0.60 = 3.20. Firm B: 0.50×2 + 0.30×3 + 0.20×4 = 1.00 + 0.90 + 0.80 = 2.70. Firm A leads, driven by the heavily weighted first factor.

💡 This is my own practice question (not from the slides); both totals are verified. Notice the weights sum to 1.0 and no two firms share a rating on any factor.
Q7: How is a CPM different from an EFE matrix, and what are the common Five-Forces pitfalls?

An EFE matrix evaluates how well the firm handles external opportunities and threats; a CPM compares the firm with named competitors on critical success factors. Five-Forces pitfalls: equal weight on all five forces, defining the industry too broadly or narrowly, and labelling the industry attractive or unattractive rather than using the model to formulate strategy.

Q8: Out of 200 customers, 90 are promoters, 40 are detractors, and 70 are passives. Compute the NPS and say what to do.

NPS = % promoters − % detractors = 45% − 20% = 25. Passives are at risk of leaving for competitors, so work on turning the 70 passives into promoters.

Glossary

Five-Forces Model
Porter's model of industry competition: rivalry, supplier power, consumer power, threat of new entrants, and threat of substitutes.
Competitive Intelligence (CI)
A systematic and ethical process for gathering and analyzing information about competitors and general business trends.
Backward Integration
Gaining control or ownership of suppliers.
Barriers to Entry
Obstacles that make it hard for new competitors to enter an industry — scale economies, capital needs, brand loyalty, regulation, patents, and similar.
Substitute Product
A product from another industry that meets the same customer need (e.g., ride-hailing for public transport).
EFE Matrix
External Factor Evaluation matrix: a weighted, rated summary of how well a firm handles external opportunities and threats.
Weighted Score
A factor's weight multiplied by its rating.
Delphi Method
A structured group technique the lecture suggests for brainstorming and ranking EFE factors.
Competitive Profile Matrix (CPM)
A matrix comparing a firm with its major competitors across weighted critical success factors.
Critical Success Factor
A key variable, tailored to the industry, on which a firm and its competitors are weighted and rated in a CPM.
Net Promoter Score (NPS)
Percentage of promoters (9–10) minus percentage of detractors (0–6), from a 1–10 recommendation scale.
