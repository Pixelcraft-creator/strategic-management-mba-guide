Defining Strategic Management

Strategic Management
The art and science of formulating, implementing, and evaluating cross-functional decisions that enable an organization to achieve its objectives.
🔤 Terms That Are Easy to Confuse

Strategic Management vs. Strategic Planning

In this course the two terms are used synonymously. Elsewhere, "strategic management" sometimes covers all three stages (formulation, implementation, evaluation) while "strategic planning" refers to formulation only.

Long-Range Planning

Tries to optimize the trends of today for tomorrow — it extends the present forward, rather than choosing among competing futures.

A strategic plan is a company's game plan. It is the outcome of tough managerial choices among numerous good alternatives, and it signals commitment to specific markets, policies, procedures, and operations. The word "cross-functional" matters: strategy is not a marketing plan or a finance plan — it cuts across all functions.

💬 Strategic Planning Can Start Informally

A firm does not need an elaborate system to begin. Informal strategic planning can start with three simple questions:

1
Where is the organization now?
Establish the current position honestly before planning anything.
2
Where will it be if nothing significant changes?
Project one, two, five, and ten years out — and ask whether those answers are acceptable.
3
If the answers are not acceptable, what specific actions should management take?
Weigh the risks and payoffs involved in each action.

The Three Stages

1 · Strategy Formulation

Deciding what to do. Develop a vision and mission; identify external opportunities and threats; determine internal strengths and weaknesses; establish long-term objectives; generate alternative strategies; choose the particular strategies to pursue.

2 · Strategy Implementation

Doing it. Often called the action stage. Establish annual objectives, devise policies, motivate employees, and allocate resources so the formulated strategies can be executed.

3 · Strategy Evaluation

Checking it worked. Determine which strategies are not working well — the only way to know whether the first two stages succeeded.

🧭 Stage 1 — Formulation Decisions

Formulation issues come down to a set of large, hard-to-reverse choices:

  • What new businesses to enter
  • What businesses to abandon
  • Whether to expand operations or diversify
  • Whether to enter international markets
  • Whether to merge or form a joint venture
  • How to avoid a hostile takeover
⚙️ Stage 2 — What Implementation Involves

Implementation turns a plan into action and touches nearly every part of the organization:

  • Developing a supportive culture
  • Creating an effective organizational structure
  • Redirecting marketing efforts
  • Preparing budgets
  • Devising tactics and other supporting tasks
Implementation is where most strategies succeed or fail in practice. A brilliant formulation with no budget, structure, or motivated people behind it is only a document.
🔍 Stage 3 — The Three Fundamental Evaluation Activities
1
Review external and internal factors
Revisit the external and internal factors that formed the basis of the current strategies — they may have changed.
2
Measure performance
Compare actual results against what the strategy was meant to deliver.
3
Take corrective actions
Adjust strategies that are not working — this feeds back into formulation.

Benefits of Strategic Management

📈 Why It Pays Off
  • Lets an organization be proactive rather than reactive in shaping its own future
  • Lets it initiate and influence activities instead of merely responding to them — exerting control over its own destiny
  • A good "fit" between the environment and the organization's strategy, structure, and processes has positive effects on performance
  • Becomes more important as the environment becomes more unstable

Financial Benefits

Organizations using strategic-management concepts show significant improvement in sales, profitability, and productivity compared with firms lacking systematic planning. High-performing firms tend to plan systematically to prepare for fluctuations in their external and internal environments.

Indirect Financial Benefits

  • Enhanced awareness of external threats
  • Improved understanding of competitors' strategies
  • Increased employee productivity
  • Reduced resistance to change
  • Clearer understanding of performance–reward relationships

Why Some Firms Do No Strategic Planning

The slides list ten reasons as a flat list. The grouping below is a study aid only — it helps you remember them by underlying cause.

Knowledge & Incentives

  • No formal training in strategic management
  • No understanding or appreciation of the benefits
  • No monetary rewards for planning
  • No punishment for not planning

Time & Priorities

  • Too busy "firefighting" (resolving internal crises) to plan ahead
  • Seen as a waste of time, since no product or service is made
  • Laziness — effective planning takes time and effort, and time is money

Complacency & Experience

  • Content with current success — not realizing today's success is no guarantee of tomorrow's
  • Overconfidence
  • Prior bad experience with strategic planning somewhere

Pitfalls in Strategic Planning

Doing strategic planning badly can be as damaging as not doing it. The slides list fourteen pitfalls; they are grouped here by theme for study (the grouping is not the slides' own).

ThemePitfall
Wrong purpose or weak commitmentUsing planning to gain control over decisions and resources
Doing planning only to satisfy accreditation or regulatory requirements
Top managers not actively supporting the planning process
Viewing planning as unnecessary or unimportant
Poor communication and involvementNot communicating the plan to employees, who keep working "in the dark"
Delegating planning to a "planner" instead of involving all managers
Not involving key employees in all phases of planning
Not creating a collaborative climate supportive of change
Viewing planning activities as silos made up of independent parts
Weak execution or wrong balanceMoving too hastily from mission development to strategy formulation
Top managers making many intuitive decisions that conflict with the formal plan
Not using the plan as a standard for measuring performance
Becoming so engrossed in current problems that little or no planning is done
Being so formal that flexibility and creativity are stifled
Notice that pitfalls exist at both extremes: too little planning (engrossed in current problems) and too much formality (flexibility and creativity stifled). Effective strategic management sits between them.

Business vs. Military Strategy

The Fundamental Difference

Business strategy is formulated, implemented, and evaluated on an assumption of competition. Military strategy is based on an assumption of conflict.

What They Share

Both business and military organizations must adapt to change and constantly improve to be successful.

📜 Sun Tzu, The Art of War — Three Ideas from the Slides
  • Vital importance: strategic planning is a matter of life or death for the state, so it must be studied thoroughly
  • Know yourself and your enemy: do that, and you will never be defeated in a hundred battles — the business parallel is the internal and external assessment
  • Flexibility: skillful leaders do not let a strategy inhibit creative counter-movement — the same warning as "too formal" in the pitfalls

Gaining & Sustaining Competitive Advantage

🔄 The Competitive-Advantage Cycle (Figure 1.4)

The textbook closes the chapter with a four-part cycle at whose centre sits the goal: gain and sustain competitive advantages. It is a loop, not a line — evaluation returns to vision and mission.

1
Establish a clear vision and mission
The starting point of the cycle.
2
Formulate strategies
Collect, analyze, and prioritize data using matrices; establish a clear strategic plan.
3
Implement strategies
Establish structure; allocate resources; motivate and reward; attract customers; manage finances.
4
Evaluate and monitor results
Take corrective actions and adapt to change — then return to step 1.

Exam Preparation

Q1: Define strategic management and name its three stages.

Strategic management is the art and science of formulating, implementing, and evaluating cross-functional decisions that enable an organization to achieve its objectives. The three stages are strategy formulation, strategy implementation, and strategy evaluation, linked by a feedback loop.

💡 Include the phrase "cross-functional" — it is the part of the definition students most often drop.
Q2: Distinguish strategic management, strategic planning, and long-range planning.

In this course strategic management and strategic planning are used synonymously, although some authors reserve "strategic planning" for formulation only. Long-range planning differs: it tries to optimize the trends of today for tomorrow, extending the present forward, whereas strategic planning involves choosing among alternatives and committing to specific markets, policies, procedures, and operations.

Q3: Explain what happens in each stage of strategic management.

Formulation: develop vision and mission, identify external opportunities and threats, determine internal strengths and weaknesses, set long-term objectives, generate alternatives, and choose strategies. Implementation (the action stage): set annual objectives, devise policies, motivate employees, and allocate resources; build culture and structure, redirect marketing, prepare budgets. Evaluation: review the external and internal bases for the current strategies, measure performance, and take corrective action.

Q4: Discuss the benefits of strategic management to an organization.

It makes the organization proactive instead of reactive and lets it influence rather than merely respond to events. A good fit between environment and strategy, structure, and processes improves performance, and planning matters more as the environment becomes less stable. Financially, planning firms show significant improvement in sales, profitability, and productivity. Indirect financial benefits: they gain awareness of external threats, understand competitors better, have more productive employees, face less resistance to change, and see clearer performance–reward links.

💡 Structure the answer as direct vs. indirect financial benefits to show command of the framework.
Q5: A firm's top managers say strategic planning is "a waste of time — we make products, not plans." Which reasons for not planning does this reflect, and how would you respond?

This reflects viewing planning as a waste of time because no product or service is made, and probably a lack of appreciation for the benefits of planning. A response: planning firms show better sales, profitability, and productivity; planning also improves awareness of threats and competitors. Because success today is no guarantee of success tomorrow, skipping planning leaves the firm reacting to events instead of shaping them.

Q6: Identify three pitfalls in strategic planning and explain how each could be avoided.

(1) Not communicating the plan to employees — employees work in the dark; avoid by communicating the plan and its rationale throughout the organization. (2) Delegating planning to a "planner" — avoid by involving all managers and key employees in every phase. (3) Being so formal that flexibility and creativity are stifled — avoid by keeping the process disciplined but adaptable to change.

💡 Any three of the fourteen work. Pair each pitfall with its opposite behaviour to give a complete answer.
Q7: Compare business strategy and military strategy.

The fundamental difference is the underlying assumption: business strategy assumes competition, military strategy assumes conflict. What they share is the need to adapt to change and constantly improve. Sun Tzu's ideas — study strategy thoroughly, know yourself and your enemy, and do not let strategy inhibit creative counter-movement — map onto internal and external assessment and the risk of over-formal planning.

Glossary

Strategic Management
The art and science of formulating, implementing, and evaluating cross-functional decisions that enable an organization to achieve its objectives.
Strategic Plan
A company's game plan: the result of tough managerial choices among numerous good alternatives, signalling commitment to specific markets, policies, procedures, and operations.
Long-Range Planning
Planning that tries to optimize the trends of today for tomorrow.
Strategy Formulation
The first stage: developing vision and mission, assessing external and internal factors, setting long-term objectives, generating alternatives, and choosing strategies.
Strategy Implementation
The "action stage": establishing annual objectives, devising policies, motivating employees, and allocating resources so formulated strategies can be executed.
Strategy Evaluation
The final stage: reviewing the bases of current strategies, measuring performance, and taking corrective action.
Feedback Loop
The link in the strategic-management model that carries evaluation results back into earlier stages so strategies can be revised.
Fit
The match between an organization's environment and its strategy, structure, and processes, which has positive effects on performance.
Firefighting
Resolving internal crises so continuously that no time remains for planning ahead — a common reason firms do not plan.
Competitive Advantage
The outcome the strategic-management cycle aims to gain and sustain, by aligning vision and mission, strategy formulation, implementation, and evaluation.
