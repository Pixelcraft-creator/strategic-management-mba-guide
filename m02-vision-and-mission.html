Where This Fits

Module 1 introduced the strategic-management model. Its first box, Business Vision and Mission (Chapter 2), is where formulation begins — before any external or internal analysis. Everything later (the external audit, strategy choice, implementation) is judged against these two statements.

Order matters: the vision answers "What do we want to become?" and is written first, because it provides the foundation for a comprehensive mission statement.

Vision Statements

Vision
A clear, specific, compelling picture of what the organization will look like at a specific time in the future (three to five years), including those few key metrics that define success (Stanford Business School, as quoted in the lecture).
Vision statement
A compelling verbal image that forms a mental picture of the future. It defines what we seek to become as an organization, should generate human energy and engagement, and gives direction and focus.
📌 Key Points the Lecture Stresses
  • A vision statement answers one question: "What do we want to become?"
  • It should be developed first, since it is the foundation for the mission statement
  • Vision statements should be written from the customer perspective
Example — Microsoft: "A computer on every desk and in every home running Microsoft software." The lecture's point is that the vision drove the whole company: everyone you met there could articulate it. It was a shared vision.
❓ Why Do We Need a Vision?

Provides direction

Spelling out the vision focuses everyone's attention on the future and determines the path to get there.

Determines decisions

It influences every decision, big or small, by executives, managers, and employees — positively or negatively, depending on how well defined it is.

Inspires people

A clear vision excites people to action; people want to work for an organization that sets its sights high.

Allows perseverance in adversity

A vision acts as a company's "guiding star," helping people overcome imposing obstacles.

Alignment

The lecture illustrates the benefit with two arrow diagrams: in one, a single small arrow points the opposite way ("this one will require special attention"); in the other, every small arrow points the same direction as the big one. A shared vision is what turns individual effort into one direction.

The 5-out-of-5 Vision Test

✅ Five Characteristics of a Good Vision Statement
#CharacteristicIt must…
1Clearreveal the industry type and what the firm strives to become
2Futuristicreveal what the firm strives to become or accomplish within 5 years
3Concisebe one sentence in length
4Uniquereveal the firm's competitive advantage
5Inspiringmotivate readers to support the firm
How the test works: score 1 for each characteristic the statement meets and 0 for each it misses. A good vision scores 5 out of 5; if it does not, evaluate it and rewrite it. The Individual Assignment's answer template uses exactly this table: Component, Score, Justification.

Mission Statements

Mission statement
An enduring statement of purpose that distinguishes one organization from other similar enterprises. A declaration of an organization's "reason for being." It answers the pivotal question "What is our business?" and is essential for effectively establishing objectives and formulating strategies.
🏷️ What It Reveals, and What It Is Also Called

A mission statement reveals what an organization wants to be and whom it wants to serve. The lecture notes it is also called a creed statement, a statement of purpose, a statement of philosophy, a statement of beliefs, and a statement of business principles.

Vision

"What do we want to become?" — future-facing, written first.

Mission

"What is our business?" — enduring reason for being, who we serve.

⚖️ Balance: Broad Enough, Not Vague
  • A good mission statement allows for the generation and consideration of a range of feasible alternative objectives and strategies without unduly stifling management creativity
  • It must be broad enough to reconcile differences among, and appeal to, an organization's diverse stakeholders

The lecture's stakeholder diagram places the organization at the centre, with two-way arrows to: employees, customers, unions, social and political action groups, shareholders, competitors, communities, trade and industry associations, suppliers, governments, and media.

🎁 Utility Statements: Sell Benefits, Not Things

Characteristic 4 (utility) rests on the lecture's "utility statements": a mission should be developed after considering the value of the firm's offerings to customers. The pattern is always the same — do not offer the thing; offer the benefit.

Do not offer me…Offer me…
ClothesAttractive looks
ShoesComfort for my feet and the pleasure of walking
A houseSecurity, comfort, and a place that is clean and happy
BooksHours of pleasure and the benefit of knowledge
CDsLeisure and the sound of music
ToolsThe benefits and pleasure that come from making beautiful things
FurnitureComfort and the quietness of a cozy place
ThingsIdeas, emotions, ambience, feelings, and benefits
Written from a customer perspective, a mission statement can spur employees, salespersons, and managers to provide exemplary service — arguably enhancing loyalty and making customers "on a mission" to seek out, use, and promote the firm's products.

Developing Vision & Mission Statements

🛠️ The Widely Used Four-Step Approach
1
Managers read the chapter
Ask all managers to read the chapter as background, and involve as many managers as possible.
2
Managers draft their own versions
Ask managers themselves to prepare a vision and mission statement for the organization.
3
A facilitator or committee merges them
A facilitator or committee of top managers merges the drafts into a single document and distributes it to all managers.
4
Request changes, then meet to revise
A request for modifications, additions, and deletions is needed next, along with a meeting to revise the document.

Worked Examples: Evaluating & Rewriting Missions

The assignment follows this exact method: (1) read the current statement; (2) tag each phrase with the component number it covers; (3) list the components that are missing; (4) rewrite so all nine appear; (5) count the words (fewer than 100). Below, every example uses the lecture's own tagging.

🥛 Nestlé — a Model Answer (Tagged Statement)

"Our philosophy at Nestlé is to hire the most talented scientists (4) and most devoted employees (9) as we strive to provide the most nutritious foods, drinks, and pet foods (2) to our customers. Quality, healthy, affordable foods (7) should be available to everyone (1) around the world (3). We are committed to controlling costs (5) and conducting business in an ethical way (6) that has a neutral impact on the environment (8)."

Result: all 9 components present. Word count: 64 — recounted independently and confirmed. Note that the lecture's tag "(4) technology" attaches to "scientists": technology is shown through the people who create it, so a component can be satisfied indirectly.
🏦 Citigroup

Current (paraphrased)

Trusted partner; financial services; connects people in hundreds of countries and cities; protects savings; advises on investing; supports governments; serves all customers responsibly and ethically, managing risk to earn the public's trust.

Missing: technology (4), survival & growth (5), employees (9).

Proposed — adds 4, 5, 9

Adds "we use banking technology" (4), "we create shareholders' value" (5) and "our employees proudly serve" (9). Tags in the lecture's proposal: 1, 2 customers/services · 3 markets · 4 technology · 5 value · 6 trust/ethics · 7 advisory expertise · 8 governments/community · 9 employees.

SOURCE VALUE vs. VERIFIED VALUE

The lecture labels the Citigroup proposal "(100 words)". Counting the words of the proposed statement as printed gives 101. Counting conventions can differ, but note the consequence: characteristic 2 requires fewer than 100, so a 100- or 101-word proposal would not strictly pass. If an answer asks you to check word count, state your counting convention.

Vision, Mission, Values, Goals, Objectives

🪜 See, Have, Believe, Pursue, Achieve
1
You SEE a Vision
A verbal image of what we ultimately want to achieve.
2
You HAVE a Mission
Your business purpose and market sector.
3
You BELIEVE in Values
Your moral compass and expected behavior.
4
You PURSUE Goals
The results we choose to accomplish. Goals guide decisions and actions, but usually do not involve measurable results, and so change less often than objectives.
5
You ACHIEVE Objectives
Measurable targets and activities along the path to the achievement of goals.

Exam Preparation

Q1: Distinguish a vision statement from a mission statement.

A vision statement answers "What do we want to become?" — a compelling picture of the organization three to five years out, written first and from the customer perspective. A mission statement answers "What is our business?" — an enduring statement of purpose and reason for being that distinguishes the firm from similar enterprises and reveals whom it wants to serve. The vision is the foundation for the mission.

Q2: List and explain the five characteristics of a good vision statement.

Clear (reveals the industry and what the firm strives to become), futuristic (what it strives to accomplish within five years), concise (one sentence), unique (reveals the firm's competitive advantage), and inspiring (motivates readers to support the firm). Scoring 1 or 0 on each gives the "5 out of 5" test.

💡 Be ready to score a given vision and justify each 0 or 1 — a bare score without a reason earns little.
Q3: Why does a firm need a vision?

It provides direction, determines decisions at every level, inspires people to action, and allows perseverance in the face of adversity as the company's "guiding star." A shared vision also aligns individual effort in one direction (the Microsoft example).

Q4: List the nine components of a mission statement.

Customers; products or services; markets; technology; concern for survival, growth, and profitability; philosophy; distinctive competence; concern for public image; concern for employees.

Q5: A mission statement says: "We will produce superior financial returns for shareowners by providing high value-added logistics services." Which components are present and which are missing?

Present: concern for survival, growth, and profitability (5) and products or services (2); "high value-added" also hints at distinctive competence (7). Missing: customers (1), markets (3), technology (4), philosophy (6), public image (8), employees (9). To fix it, rewrite to add the missing components while staying under 100 words.

💡 Methodical tagging, phrase by phrase, is how the lecture answers every such question.
Q6: List five characteristics of an effective mission statement.

Any five of: broad in scope with no numbers or objectives; fewer than 100 words; inspiring; identifies the utility of the firm's products; reveals social responsibility; reveals environmental responsibility; includes the nine components; reconciliatory; enduring; attracts customers.

Q7: Explain the "See, Have, Believe, Pursue, Achieve" sequence.

You see a vision (a verbal image of what we ultimately want), have a mission (business purpose and market sector), believe in values (moral compass and expected behavior), pursue goals (results we choose to accomplish — usually not measurable and slower to change), and achieve objectives (measurable targets along the path to the goals).

Glossary

Vision Statement
A compelling verbal image of what the organization seeks to become, typically three to five years out; answers "What do we want to become?"
Mission Statement
An enduring statement of purpose that distinguishes one organization from similar enterprises; answers "What is our business?"
5-out-of-5 Test
Scoring a vision statement 1 or 0 on each of five characteristics — clear, futuristic, concise, unique, inspiring.
Utility Statement
A statement of the benefit a firm's products give customers, rather than a description of the products themselves.
Stakeholders
The groups with an interest in the organization — employees, customers, shareholders, suppliers, communities, governments, unions, media, competitors, trade associations, and social and political action groups.
Reconciliatory
A characteristic of a mission statement: broad enough to reconcile differences among diverse stakeholders.
Distinctive Competence
The firm's major competitive advantage — mission component 7.
Core Values
The moral compass and expected behavior of an organization — what you "believe in" in the vision–mission–values–goals–objectives sequence.
Goal
A result the organization chooses to accomplish; guides decisions and actions but usually does not involve measurable results.
Objective
A measurable target or activity along the path to achieving a goal.
